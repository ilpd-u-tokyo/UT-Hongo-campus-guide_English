STOP 1 · START

Sanjo Conference Hall

山上会館

Where Sanjo Conference Hall stands today, there was once the Hongo main residence of the Kaga Domain. In the Meiji era, the "Bekka Medical School," the predecessor of the University of Tokyo Faculty of Medicine, was placed here. Later, this building was moved to a high ground overlooking Sanshiro Pond and came to be called "Sanjo Goten." A goten is a grand, prestigious mansion where people of high social status live. Sanjo Goten was used as the university headquarters and a conference hall, but it was destroyed by fire in the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake.

And now: standing in this historic location is the current Sanjo Conference Hall. To commemorate the 100th anniversary of the founding of the University of Tokyo, it was built in 1986, designed by the architect Kunio Maekawa.

≈ 80 m · 2 min walk to Sanshiro Pond
STOP 2

Sanshiro Pond

三四郎池

Its official name is "Ikutokuen Shinji-ike." Originally, it was part of a garden built in the estate of the Maeda family, lords of the Kaga Domain. During the Edo period, it was known as a "stroll garden" where visitors could enjoy the scenery while walking around the pond, and it was considered one of the famous gardens of the time.

The name "Shinji-ike" (Heart-Character Pond) has several origins. It is said that the name was given because the shape of the pond resembles the character for "heart" (心) when viewed from above, and because in Zen thinking, a pond was believed to reflect the human heart.

So, why is it called "Sanshiro Pond"? The catalyst was the novel Sanshiro by Natsume Soseki, who was also a graduate of the University of Tokyo. In the novel, this pond appears in the scene where the main character, Sanshiro, meets Mineko.

Showing a different expression in each season, such as the fresh green of spring and the autumn leaves, Sanshiro Pond offers much to see. Please pause for a moment and enjoy the rich nature.

≈ 120 m · 3 min walk to Yasuda Auditorium
STOP 3

Yasuda Auditorium

安田講堂

Its official name is the "Grand Auditorium," and it was completed in 1925. The construction cost was covered by a donation from the businessman Zenjiro Yasuda. In fact, Yasuda made the donation at the time without revealing his name. It was only after Yasuda passed away that his contribution became known. Since then, in memory of him, this building has come to be called "Yasuda Auditorium."

Please also pay attention to the arch at the main entrance.

There are still scars left from the "Todai Conflict" that occurred between 1968 and 1969. They are intentionally preserved without restoration to convey the history of the University of Tokyo. Yasuda Auditorium can accommodate about 1,000 people and is still used today for events representing the university, such as entrance and graduation ceremonies.
≈ 150 m · 3 min walk to Ginkgo Avenue
STOP 4

Faculty of Law & Letters Bldg. 1 / Ginkgo Avenue

法文１号館・イチョウ並木

This Ginkgo Avenue was developed around 1906. The president at the time, Arata Hamao, wanted to create a campus with a solemn atmosphere that would naturally make people straighten their collars the moment they entered the Main Gate. Therefore, it is said that after consulting with Professor Seiroku Honda of the College of Agriculture, ginkgo trees were planted instead of cherry trees. This avenue survived World War II without being destroyed by fire and still remains today.

In autumn, the leaves turn a vivid yellow, creating a beautiful landscape representing the Hongo Campus. On the other hand, ginkgo nuts have a unique smell, and students sometimes express complaints about them — even a beautiful avenue has its minor troubles.

And please also pay attention to the arch of Faculty of Law and Letters Bldg. 1 visible in front of you. It is known for its beautiful design and serves as the motif for the logo mark of the University of Tokyo Faculty of Law.

≈ 130 m · 2 min walk to the General Library
STOP 5

General Library

総合図書館

The current building was completed in 1928 and was designed by the architect Yoshikazu Uchida. A library had been in this location previously, but it was destroyed by fire in the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake. The current General Library was rebuilt after that.

Please pay attention to the exterior of the building. A design reminiscent of the spines of books lined up on a bookshelf has been incorporated. The current collection consists of over 1.3 million volumes — about 40 to 65 times that of a typical high school library.

And there are also highlights inside the building: ammonite fossils can be seen on the pillars, and there is a huge "automated storage facility" in the basement. When you enter the call number of the book you need, a machine searches for the book and automatically transports it. The interesting part of the University of Tokyo's General Library is that such state-of-the-art equipment is also provided inside a historic building.
≈ 90 m · 2 min walk to Fukutake Hall
STOP 6

Fukutake Hall "Thinking Wall" & Glass Building (School of Law)

考える壁、ガラス棟

Beyond this point, there are buildings by two Japanese architects who have won the "Pritzker Prize," often called the Nobel Prize of the architecture world.

First, please look at this long concrete wall. It is the "Thinking Wall," designed by the architect Tadao Ando. It is said to have been inspired by Sanjusangendo in Kyoto. Rather than closing off the space, this wall creates a "margin" (ma) that gently connects the campus with the underground space beyond the wall. Please take a look beyond the wall as well.

And the glass building in the back on the right is the Law School. This was designed by the architect Fumihiko Maki. It features a simple and flexible space that can adapt to future changes. The translucent glass exterior expresses the image of an open university.

Tadao Ando and Fumihiko Maki: please pay attention to the architectural individuality of each.

≈ 100 m · 2 min walk to the Red Gate
STOP 7 · FINAL STOP

Red Gate — Akamon

赤門（懐徳門）

Built in 1827, it is the oldest surviving building on campus. Originally, this gate belonged to the Maeda family, lords of the Kaga Domain, which once occupied most of the Hongo Campus.

This gate was built when Princess Yasu, daughter of the 11th Shogun Tokugawa Ienari, married Maeda Nariyasu, the lord of the Kaga Domain. Gates built for the daughters of shoguns were often demolished after the princesses passed away. However, Princess Yasu lived a long life, and Akamon survived.

If you look closely, there is the Tokugawa family crest on the roof tiles, and below it, the Maeda family crest is depicted. Characters meaning "learning" can also be seen on the decorative roof tiles — they were added in the Meiji era. With a history of about 200 years, Akamon is one of the most valuable buildings that reminds us of the past of the Edo period.
